Recognizing the pattern

CADASIL symptoms & signs

CADASIL symptoms vary widely. CADASIL can look very different from one person to another. Certain symptoms are more commonly reported at particular stages of life, but there is no universal sequence or timeline. Some people experience several manifestations of CADASIL; others remain mildly affected for decades.

Commonly reported patterns

How CADASIL can show up.

Five overlapping symptom domains have been reported in CADASIL. Not everyone experiences all of them, and no two courses look exactly alike.

Migraine with aura

Often the earliest symptom — appearing in the 20s or 30s. About 30–40% of CADASIL patients experience migraine with aura. Auras may be visual, sensory, or aphasic, and are sometimes prolonged (hours rather than minutes), atypical, or hemiplegic.

Rarely, a migraine attack can be followed by an encephalopathic episode — hours to days of fever, seizures, altered consciousness, or severe migraine-like symptoms. These are frightening but generally reversible, and always warrant emergency assessment. Learn more →

Onset: 20s–30s · Frequency: 30–40% of patients

Stroke & TIA

Recurrent lacunar (small, deep) ischemic strokes and TIAs are the clinical hallmark — most often beginning between the ages of 40 and 70. They typically occur in patients without classical vascular risk factors, which is part of why diagnosis is delayed.

In plain terms, a CADASIL stroke often looks like sudden one-sided weakness (arm, leg, or face), slurred speech, or a brief episode of numbness or vision change. Individual events are usually mild, and many people recover most or all of their function. But the cumulative effect of repeated strokes over years is what drives the disease's long-term impact — which is why early diagnosis and vascular risk-factor control matter so much.

Onset: 40s–70s · Frequency: 60–85% over a lifetime

Cognitive impairment

When cognitive change occurs in CADASIL, it commonly affects attention, processing speed, and executive function first. Memory tends to be relatively spared early on. Some people experience stepwise change rather than smooth decline; others report only subtle changes for many years, or none at all.

Age most commonly reported: 50s–70s · Frequency: Reported in a substantial share of people with CADASIL; not universal

Mood disturbance & apathy

Depression and apathy affect ~30% of patients and may precede other symptoms. Apathy in CADASIL is a distinct clinical feature, not just "low mood" — it reflects disconnection of frontal–subcortical circuits and often goes unrecognized.

Onset: Any age · Frequency: ~30%

Gait disturbance

Later in life, some people with CADASIL develop a small-stepped, magnetic gait, imbalance, or pseudobulbar features (emotional incontinence, dysphagia). These have been linked to cumulative deep white-matter and basal-ganglia change. Not everyone develops gait problems.

Age most commonly reported: 60s+ · Frequency: More commonly seen with advanced disease; not universal

A telling family history

The pattern is autosomal dominant — strokes, "early dementia," migraines, or psychiatric illness across multiple generations. Some families have already been told their relatives had MS, a "weak vessel," or unexplained early decline.

Inheritance: 50% per child · Penetrance: Near-complete, variable expressivity

Commonly reported patterns*

Patterns across the lifespan.

This is a rough guide, not a schedule. Some people have their first stroke in their 30s. Others are diagnosed for the first time in their 70s. Some carriers in their 50s and 60s report no symptoms at all. Individual expression varies enormously — even between siblings who inherit the same variant.

* A note before you read this timeline

Timelines like this describe what has been observed on average in reported cases. They are not a forecast for any individual. Two people with the exact same NOTCH3 variant can have very different courses — and many people diagnosed with CADASIL live well beyond the ranges shown here, with function preserved for decades.

  • Age 20–30

    Migraine with aura may appear

    Migraine with aura is one of the more commonly reported early manifestations. It is often dismissed or misclassified for years — and it is not experienced by everyone with CADASIL.

  • Age 30–45

    Subtle mood or cognitive change can begin

    Some people report apathy, low motivation, depression, or mild executive difficulty in this range. These changes are frequently first attributed to life stress or burnout.

  • Age 40–55

    First TIA or lacunar stroke can occur

    For many people who are diagnosed later, a stroke or TIA is what brings CADASIL onto the differential. White matter changes are often present on MRI by this stage.

  • Age 50–70

    Recurrent events and vascular cognitive change are more commonly reported

    Some people experience clusters of events with periods of relative plateau in between. Others report a very different course.

  • Age 60+

    Gait or communication changes can occur

    A subset of people develop small-stepped gait, dysphagia, pseudobulbar features, or advanced cognitive impairment. Many people remain functionally independent into much later life.

  • Throughout life

    Wide variability between individuals

    Some carriers remain mildly affected into their 70s. Vascular risk factor control, smoking history, and likely other genetic and environmental factors influence individual course.

When should CADASIL be considered?

Clusters of features — especially in younger adults.

No single symptom on this list means a person has CADASIL. Migraine, mood change, and small white-matter spots are common on their own. CADASIL becomes more "thinkable" when several features occur together, particularly in a younger adult — and especially when there is a suggestive family history.

  • Stroke or TIA in a younger adult, especially without classical vascular risk factors
  • Migraine with aura in the 20s–30s, particularly if prolonged, atypical, or hemiplegic
  • Multiple family members with stroke, "early dementia," or unexplained neurological illness across generations
  • Confluent subcortical white-matter changes on MRI before age 50
  • Anterior temporal pole or external capsule white-matter involvement on FLAIR
  • Apathy out of proportion to depression, especially with subcortical MRI findings
  • Suspected MS with atypical features and negative CSF / oligoclonal bands

Deciding whether further evaluation is appropriate — including any genetic testing discussion — is a clinical judgement made together with a healthcare professional.

What CADASIL is not

Symptoms that are not typical of CADASIL.

Knowing what doesn't fit is just as important as knowing what does. The following features should prompt consideration of alternative diagnoses.

Typical of CADASIL

  • Subcortical white-matter MRI lesions, especially anterior temporal poles and external capsules
  • Recurrent lacunar strokes / TIAs
  • Migraine with aura — often the first sign
  • Slow, stepwise vascular cognitive impairment
  • Multi-generational family history

Atypical for CADASIL

  • Optic neuritis or recurrent inflammatory CNS attacks (suggests MS)
  • Predominantly cortical strokes from large-vessel atherosclerosis
  • Early prominent memory loss with hippocampal atrophy (suggests Alzheimer's)
  • Marked peripheral neuropathy (consider Fabry, mitochondrial, other)
  • Spinal cord lesions on MRI
