The complete guide

What is CADASIL?

CADASIL is the most common inherited cause of stroke in adults — a progressive disease of the brain's small blood vessels caused by mutations in the NOTCH3 gene. This guide explains the genetics, pathology, symptoms, prevalence, and natural history in clear, evidence-informed language.

A clear definition

CADASIL is an acronym for Cerebral Autosomal Dominant Arteriopathy with Subcortical Infarcts and Leukoencephalopathy. Each piece of the name describes the disease:

  • Cerebral — affects the brain.
  • Autosomal Dominant — inherited from one affected parent; each child has a 50% risk.
  • Arteriopathy — a disease of the arteries, specifically the small ones.
  • Subcortical Infarcts — small strokes deep beneath the cortex.
  • Leukoencephalopathy — damage to the white matter visible on MRI.

Clinically, CADASIL is the most common monogenic (single-gene) cause of stroke and vascular dementia. It is classified as a hereditary cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD), with the small arteries supplying the deep brain tissue progressively failing over decades.

Plain-language summary

CADASIL is a genetic disease that damages the smallest blood vessels in the brain. Over time it causes migraines, strokes, mood changes, and memory problems. It runs in families. There is no cure yet, but recognition, vascular risk control, and supportive care change outcomes — and research is moving forward.

The genetics: NOTCH3 and chromosome 19

CADASIL is caused by changes (variants) in the NOTCH3 gene, located on chromosome 19. NOTCH3 gives the body instructions for making a protein that supports the muscle cells wrapping the small arteries — including the small arteries deep in the brain. When those instructions are altered, the protein doesn't fold the right way, misshapen protein slowly builds up in and around the artery walls, and over years those arteries stop working the way they should.

Two things are worth knowing:

  • Where in the gene the change happens can matter. Some locations are associated with more severe disease that starts earlier; others are associated with milder, later-onset disease. This is one of the big reasons CADASIL can look so different from one family to another — and even from one person to another in the same family.
  • Having the variant does not predict a specific life course. Individual expression varies enormously, even in people who share the exact same variant. Genetics can tell you the diagnosis; it can't hand you a timeline.
The scientific details (for clinicians and researchers)

Most CADASIL-causing variants are missense changes that add or remove a cysteine residue in one of the 34 epidermal growth factor-like repeat (EGFr) domains in the extracellular portion of NOTCH3. Cysteines in these domains normally pair to form disulfide bonds critical for correct folding; a pathogenic variant leaves an unpaired cysteine, driving misfolding, extracellular aggregation of the NOTCH3 ectodomain, and toxicity to vascular smooth muscle cells.

Variants in EGFr domains 1–6 are typically associated with classical, severe CADASIL, while variants in domains 7–34 tend to associate with later-onset, milder phenotypes. This correlation is important but not absolute — expressivity remains highly variable even for identical variants. More than 270 distinct pathogenic NOTCH3 variants have been reported worldwide; some (R169C, R544C) recur in specific populations and have been studied in detail for their genotype–phenotype correlations.

Variant location matters — but doesn't determine outcome

Even the classical/severe versus mild pattern is a tendency, not a rule. Genetic counselling frames predictions probabilistically, not deterministically — because that's how the biology actually behaves.

How CADASIL is inherited

CADASIL is autosomal dominant, meaning a single copy of a pathogenic NOTCH3 variant is sufficient to cause disease. Practically, this means:

  • Each child of a parent with CADASIL has a 50% chance of inheriting the variant.
  • Both males and females are affected equally.
  • Inheriting the variant is not the same as having a predictable disease course. In genetics this is called expressivity — how the variant is expressed in each person. Two siblings who inherit the exact same NOTCH3 variant can have very different courses: one might be significantly affected at 45 while another remains nearly asymptomatic at 70. Expressivity depends on the specific variant, likely on other genes and environmental factors, and on some things we don't yet fully understand.
  • The disease is typically traceable across multiple generations, although milder variants can appear to "skip" generations because of that variable expressivity, late onset, or earlier deaths from unrelated causes.
  • De novo (new) NOTCH3 variants are rare but documented, so absence of a clear family history does not rule out CADASIL.

The biology: what's happening in the vessels

The cellular and molecular consequence of NOTCH3 misfolding is the accumulation of granular osmiophilic material (GOM) — pathognomonic deposits in the basement membrane around vascular smooth muscle cells. GOM, together with extracellular accumulation of the NOTCH3 ectodomain (NOTCH3ECD), drives:

  • Loss of vascular smooth muscle cells
  • Thickening and fibrosis of the small artery walls
  • Impaired vasoreactivity and cerebral autoregulation
  • Chronic hypoperfusion of deep white matter
  • Recurrent lacunar (small, deep) infarcts
  • Microbleeds and progressive white-matter injury

CADASIL is a systemic small-vessel disease — GOM has been demonstrated in skin, muscle, and other tissues. The brain dominates the clinical picture because of its extreme dependence on the perfusion supplied by these small, end-arterial vessels.

How common is CADASIL?

The clinical prevalence of CADASIL has been estimated at roughly 2–5 per 100,000 adults, but this almost certainly understates how common NOTCH3 variants actually are. Population genomic data — for example from the UK Biobank — suggest that pathogenic NOTCH3 variants may be present in approximately 1 in 300 to 1 in 400 individuals.

The gap between those two numbers is important — and, we think, a reason for hope. Most of the people who carry a NOTCH3 variant but have not been clinically diagnosed have very few symptoms, or none at all. Many will never develop severe disease. The heavy stories that dominate online forums and social groups are, statistically, a self-selected minority — they aren't representative of what happens to most people who carry a variant. Severe cases exist and are real; they are also a smaller share of the whole than a Google search would suggest.

The practical implication: earlier identification, informed genetic counselling, and standard management of modifiable vascular risk factors are widely regarded as the most helpful current steps for someone with a NOTCH3 variant.

Commonly reported patterns across the lifespan

There is no single CADASIL timeline

CADASIL varies considerably from person to person — even among members of the same family who share the exact same NOTCH3 variant. Some people develop symptoms relatively early, while others experience mild symptoms or remain largely unaffected well into later life. The ages and patterns below describe trends observed across groups of people with CADASIL; they do not predict what will happen to any individual.

  • 20s–30s

    Migraine with aura may appear

    Migraine with aura is one of the earlier manifestations reported in CADASIL, sometimes more prolonged or atypical than common migraine. About 30–40% of people with CADASIL experience migraine with aura; many do not.

  • 30s–50s

    Mood or cognitive changes may begin

    Some people report apathy, depression, or subtle executive-function difficulties that emerge without obvious explanation. These changes are sometimes first attributed to life stress.

  • 40s–70s

    Stroke and TIA are more commonly reported

    Recurrent lacunar strokes and TIAs are a common part of the picture in this age range, and often occur without classical vascular risk factors. Not everyone with CADASIL has strokes.

  • 50s–70s

    Vascular cognitive impairment can occur

    Some people develop stepwise change in attention, processing speed, and executive function. A smaller share develop frank dementia, gait disturbance, or pseudobulbar features. Others remain functionally independent into much later life.

  • Throughout

    Wide variability between individuals

    Modifiers — vascular risk factors, lifestyle, likely other genetic and environmental factors — matter. Individual expression can vary considerably even within a single family.

CADASIL versus other diseases

CADASIL is regularly mistaken for other neurological conditions. Knowing the differences is essential.

Multiple sclerosis (MS)MS lesions are typically periventricular, juxtacortical, or in the corpus callosum, with active enhancement and CSF oligoclonal bands. Prognosis and long-term outlook

Long-term outcome in CADASIL varies considerably from person to person. Many individuals live independently for decades after diagnosis with good function; others have a more challenging course. Individual outcome cannot be reliably predicted from genetic testing alone.

General principles of care that may support long-term function include:

  • Managing modifiable vascular risk factors (blood pressure, smoking, diabetes, lipids) as clinicians would for anyone at elevated stroke risk
  • Discussing individual medication considerations with a CADASIL-aware clinician — including any specific cautions relevant to a particular person's history
  • Migraine care in consultation with a headache specialist where symptoms warrant
  • Engagement with multidisciplinary care as needed: neurology, genetics, mental health, rehabilitation

CADASIL-specific interventions proven to reverse or stop disease progression are not yet available. Research to identify such treatments is ongoing.

Myths and misconceptions

Myth: CADASIL is too rare to consider.

Genomic data suggest pathogenic NOTCH3 variants are far more common than the reported clinical prevalence. Many people with CADASIL never receive the diagnosis.

Reality: It should be on the differential for any unexplained stroke under 60.

Especially when paired with migraine with aura, mood/cognitive change, or family history.

Myth: A normal MRI rules it out.

MRI findings can vary in CADASIL, and presymptomatic carriers may have minimal or subtle imaging changes.

Reality: MRI and genetic testing serve different roles.

MRI is an important part of the evaluation and helps clinicians investigate other possible explanations for symptoms. Identification of a pathogenic NOTCH3 variant is what confirms CADASIL — ideally in the context of informed clinical and genetic counselling.

Myth: There's nothing to do because there's no cure.

There isn't yet a treatment proven to reverse or stop CADASIL, but clinical care still matters.

Reality: A diagnosis can inform management.

Care generally focuses on managing symptoms, addressing modifiable vascular risk factors, applying established stroke-prevention principles where appropriate, and connecting with family counselling, mental-health support, and research opportunities.
